Method

How we measure

One AI answer is a snapshot, and the next one can be different. So we ask the same questions many times, in the six places people now get answers, and count what comes back. Here's exactly how, and the rules we hold ourselves to.

More than 50,000 asks so far · 6 platforms

Five steps

  1. 1

    Start with how customers really ask

    Before a study, we gather evidence of how customers word their questions: what they write in their own words, what they search for, and what the people who take their calls hear every day. Each need is then asked in a few different ways, from a short search like "best dentist in Belleville" to a full question with personal detail, because a small change in phrasing can change the answer.

    A small pilot shows whether the wording matters before the questions are locked. If it does, that's a finding, and we report it. When you commission a study, you see the questions before the main run. Our newer published studies list every question we asked, so anyone can judge them.

  2. 2

    Ask the six places people get answers

    Four AI assistants and two parts of Google, because they don't agree with each other and people use all of them.

    ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Perplexity

    The AI assistants. We ask the versions people get in the free apps, with web search switched on, so the AI looks things up just as it does for a real person. Where a platform lets us, we tell it which city the person is asking from.

    Google's AI Overview

    The AI-written answer Google shows at the top of some searches. Google decides when to show one, and the wording matters: a full question usually gets one, a few keywords often don't. We count it only when Google showed it.

    Google's map results

    The short list of places, usually three, that Google shows on a map for local searches. We record which places were listed. They often differ from the places the AIs name.

    We ask the assistants through each company's own developer access, record which version answered every time, and keep the same version through a study. Google's results come from real Google searches, run from a location we set for each study.

  3. 3

    Ask over and over, spread out

    Each question is asked many times, across several days and times of day, so no single moment decides the result. Even with nothing changed, the answers shift a little from ask to ask. That wobble is the noise floor: asking many times lets us measure it, so a change only counts when it's bigger than the wobble.

  4. 4

    Keep every answer, and every source

    Every answer is stored in full, with every website it cited. The AIs are free to name anyone. Afterwards, we match every name against the businesses in that market, so a name counts whether it's written "Dr. Smith" or "Smith Family Dental".

    Then a person checks a sample of answers by hand against what we counted. When the check finds a miss, we fix the matching and recount everything.

  5. 5

    Count honestly

    A rate is the number of answers that named you, divided by the number of times we actually asked, counted from the data, never assumed. A business counts once per answer, however many times the answer names it. Google's AI Overview is only counted when Google actually showed one.

Why we ask so many times

One answer tells you very little. Hundreds tell you how often a business is really named. That's why every study asks each question many times, and why we've asked more than 50,000 times so far.

Rules we hold ourselves to

We measure. We don't predict.

We once built a score that tried to predict how visible a business would be from its website. Our own measurements showed it didn't work, so we dropped it. Now we only report what the AIs actually say.

We decide the test before we see the results.

Questions are locked before a study starts. For larger studies, we also write down how we'll judge the results in advance, so the data can't be bent to fit a story.

We recount before we publish.

Before a study goes live, a separate check recounts every figure on the page from the raw answers. If a sentence says more than the data shows, we change the sentence, not the data.

We label every change.

Each answer records which AI version gave it. When a platform changes, or we change a setting, new results are labelled separately and never quietly blended with old ones.

We check our own tools.

Automatic checks run on every batch, for example flagging any answer that was cut short. When we find a problem, we fix it, re-measure, and say so.

Nobody pays to be in a study.

We don't sell fixes, rankings or placements, and we never charge anyone to appear in our results.

Each business's detail stays with that business.

Our published studies show patterns. We name a business only where it comes off well or the fact is already public, such as a closure. A business's own detail, like which questions it's named for and what the sources say about it, goes only to that business.

What we can't see

The answer any one person gets can differ from what we measure. Their location, their history with the app and their settings can all change it. We ask through the platforms' developer access, which works like the app but isn't identical to it, and Gemini can't be told where the person is. We measure the standard answer, many times over, which is the fairest common picture there is. And because the AIs change often, every result is dated: it describes what the platforms said at the time we asked.
